01What Is a Nucleotide?

DNA and RNA are both built from small repeating units called nucleotides. A nucleotide is a molecule made of three parts snapped together: a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. To understand DNA, you need to understand each piece.

The Sugar

The sugar in a nucleotide is a five-carbon sugar (a pentose). The five carbons are numbered 1′ through 5′ (read as "one prime" through "five prime"). The prime marks are just a naming convention used to distinguish the sugar carbons from the carbons in the base. In DNA, the sugar is deoxyribose. In RNA, the sugar is ribose. The only structural difference between them is at the 2′ carbon: deoxyribose has just a hydrogen there (it is "de-oxy," meaning it is missing one oxygen), while ribose has a hydroxyl group (-OH). This seemingly tiny difference makes DNA more chemically stable than RNA, which is one reason DNA is used for long-term information storage.

The Phosphate Group

A phosphate group is a phosphorus atom bonded to four oxygen atoms (PO43−). It carries a negative charge at biological pH. The phosphate attaches to the 5′ carbon of the sugar. When nucleotides link together into a chain, the phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the 3′ carbon of the next nucleotide's sugar, creating a phosphodiester bond. This bond is a covalent bond (strong, within the backbone), not a hydrogen bond. The repeating sugar-phosphate-sugar-phosphate chain forms the backbone of the DNA strand.

The Nitrogenous Base

The base is a ring-shaped molecule containing nitrogen. It attaches to the 1′ carbon of the sugar. The base is the part that varies from nucleotide to nucleotide and carries the genetic information. There are four bases in DNA: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C), and thymine (T). The sequence of these bases along the strand is what encodes genetic instructions.

Key Point
Every nucleotide has the same sugar and phosphate. The only thing that differs is the base. So the "genetic alphabet" is really just four letters: A, G, C, and T. When we write a DNA sequence like ATCGGA, we are listing the bases in order along one strand of the backbone.
Nucleotide = 3 Parts
Pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) + Phosphate group (on 5′ carbon) + Nitrogenous base (on 1′ carbon)
Common Mistake
Students often confuse "nucleotide" with "nucleoside." A nucleoside is just the sugar plus the base (no phosphate). A nucleotide is the sugar plus the base plus at least one phosphate group. If there is no phosphate, it is a nucleoside, not a nucleotide. ATP (adenosine triphosphate), for example, is a nucleotide: it has ribose, adenine, and three phosphates.

02Purines & Pyrimidines

The four DNA bases fall into two structural categories based on the size of their ring structure. This distinction matters because it directly determines how base pairing works.

Purines have a double-ring structure (a six-membered ring fused to a five-membered ring). The two purines in DNA are adenine (A) and guanine (G). They are the larger bases.

Pyrimidines have a single-ring structure (one six-membered ring). The pyrimidines in DNA are cytosine (C) and thymine (T). In RNA, thymine is replaced by uracil (U), which is also a pyrimidine. Pyrimidines are the smaller bases.

Memory Tricks

"Pure As Gold" = Purines are Adenine and Guanine.

"CUT the Py" = Pyrimidines are Cytosine, Uracil, Thymine.

Or remember: pYrimidine has a Y, and so does Cytosine and Thymine.

Why This Matters
Base pairing always joins one purine to one pyrimidine: one big ring pairs with one small ring. This keeps the width of the DNA double helix constant from one rung to the next. If two purines paired, the rung would be too wide. If two pyrimidines paired, it would be too narrow. The consistent purine-pyrimidine pairing gives DNA its uniform diameter of 2 nanometers.

03Base Pairing & Chargaff's Rules

The two strands of DNA are held together by hydrogen bonds between the bases. But the pairing is not random. Each base can only pair with one specific partner. This is called complementary base pairing.

Base Pairing Rules
A pairs with T — held by 2 hydrogen bonds
G pairs with C — held by 3 hydrogen bonds

Why These Specific Pairs?

Two reasons work together. First, size: a purine must pair with a pyrimidine (one large + one small) to maintain the constant width of the helix. This eliminates A-G and C-T pairings. Second, hydrogen bonding geometry: the positions of the hydrogen bond donors and acceptors on each base are arranged so that A fits perfectly with T (two hydrogen bonds line up), and G fits perfectly with C (three hydrogen bonds line up). The wrong pairs (A-C or G-T) cannot form the right number of hydrogen bonds at the right angles, so they are energetically unfavorable. The specificity comes from both the size constraint and the chemical complementarity of the hydrogen bonding groups.

Cross-Disciplinary Bridge
The hydrogen bonds here are the same type of intermolecular force you studied in the water module. A δ+ hydrogen on an N-H or O-H group is attracted to a lone pair on a nearby nitrogen or oxygen. In water, this force holds water molecules together. In DNA, the same force holds the two strands together. The difference is specificity: in DNA, the donors and acceptors are precisely positioned by the ring structures of the bases, enforcing the A-T and G-C rules. The chemistry is identical.

Chargaff's Rules

In the late 1940s, before the structure of DNA was known, Erwin Chargaff analyzed DNA from many organisms and noticed a pattern: the amount of adenine always equaled the amount of thymine, and the amount of guanine always equaled the amount of cytosine. This is written as %A = %T and %G = %C. These are Chargaff's rules, and they follow directly from complementary base pairing. If every A on one strand pairs with a T on the other, there must be equal numbers of each.

Worked Example

A sample of double-stranded DNA is analyzed and found to contain 22% adenine. What are the percentages of the other three bases?

If %A = 22%, then %T = 22% (Chargaff's rule). That accounts for 44%. The remaining 56% is split equally between G and C: %G = 28%, %C = 28%.

Key Point
G-C base pairs have 3 hydrogen bonds; A-T pairs have 2. DNA with a higher G-C content has more hydrogen bonds per base pair, which means it takes more energy (higher temperature) to separate the two strands. This is why G-C rich DNA has a higher melting temperature (Tm). This concept shows up in molecular biology lab techniques like PCR primer design.

04The Double Helix

In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick (using X-ray crystallography data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins) proposed the double helix model of DNA. Two polynucleotide strands wind around each other in a spiral. The sugar-phosphate backbones are on the outside, like the railings of a spiral staircase. The bases face inward, forming the "steps" of the staircase, held together by hydrogen bonds.

Antiparallel Orientation

The two strands run in opposite directions. Remember that each sugar has a 5′ carbon and a 3′ carbon. Along a strand, the backbone runs from the 5′ end to the 3′ end. In the double helix, one strand runs 5′→3′ while its partner runs 3′→5′. They are antiparallel. This is not decorative; it is functionally essential. The enzymes that copy DNA (DNA polymerases) can only work in one direction: they add nucleotides to the 3′ end. The antiparallel arrangement means the two strands must be copied by different strategies during replication.

Why 5′ and 3′ Matter
The 5′ and 3′ labels come from the carbon atoms on the sugar. The 5′ carbon is where the phosphate group is attached. The 3′ carbon is where the next nucleotide connects (via a phosphodiester bond). When we say DNA is synthesized "5′ to 3′," we mean new nucleotides are always added to the free 3′ end of the growing strand, never to the 5′ end. Every enzyme involved in building nucleic acids follows this rule.

05DNA vs. RNA

DNA and RNA are both nucleic acids built from nucleotides, but they differ in several important ways. Understanding these differences helps explain why DNA stores information while RNA carries it out.

BasesA, T, G, CA, U, G, C

StabilityVery stable (long-term storage)Less stable (temporary use)

Why Uracil Instead of Thymine in RNA?

Uracil and thymine are very similar structurally. Thymine is essentially uracil with an extra methyl group (-CH3). DNA uses thymine because the methyl group makes it easier for DNA repair enzymes to distinguish thymine from cytosine that has been accidentally deaminated (damaged) into uracil. If DNA used uracil normally, the repair machinery could not tell the difference between a normal base and a damaged one. RNA does not need this repair system because it is short-lived and disposable.

06DNA Replication

Before a cell divides, it must copy all of its DNA so that each daughter cell receives a complete set. This copying process is DNA replication.

Semiconservative

Replication is semiconservative, meaning each new DNA molecule contains one original ("parent") strand and one newly synthesized ("daughter") strand. This was proven by the Meselson-Stahl experiment in 1958 using heavy nitrogen isotopes to label the original DNA and track it through rounds of replication.

The Replication Fork

Replication begins at specific sites called origins of replication and proceeds in both directions (bidirectional). The point where the two strands are being separated and new DNA is being built is called the replication fork.

Why There Is a Leading and a Lagging Strand

DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides in the 5′→3′ direction, and the two template strands are antiparallel. This creates an asymmetry. One template strand (the 3′→5′ template) can be read continuously as the fork opens, producing the leading strand in one smooth run. The other template strand (the 5′→3′ template) runs the "wrong way," so DNA polymerase must work in short segments moving away from the fork, then jump back as more template is exposed. These short segments are called Okazaki fragments, and the strand built from them is the lagging strand.

Common Mistake
Students often say "DNA polymerase reads the template 5′ to 3′." This is backward. DNA polymerase reads the template 3′→5′ and builds the new strand 5′→3′. The direction you say depends on which strand you are talking about: the template being read or the new strand being built. The new strand is always synthesized 5′→3′.

07The Central Dogma

The central dogma of molecular biology describes the flow of genetic information in a cell:

Central Dogma
DNA → (transcription) → RNA → (translation) → Protein

DNA is the master blueprint stored in the nucleus. It is never directly read by the protein-building machinery. Instead, a working copy is made in the form of messenger RNA (mRNA) through a process called transcription. The mRNA then travels out of the nucleus to ribosomes in the cytoplasm, where it is read and used to assemble a chain of amino acids into a protein through a process called translation.

Think of it like a construction project. DNA is the master blueprint locked in the architect's office (the nucleus). You do not take the master blueprint to the construction site. Instead, you make a photocopy (mRNA) and send that to the builders (ribosomes), who use it to construct the building (protein).

Key Point
Information flows in one direction: DNA → RNA → Protein. Proteins are never reverse-translated back into RNA, and RNA is not normally reverse-transcribed back into DNA (with the notable exception of retroviruses like HIV, which use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to copy RNA into DNA).

08Transcription

Transcription is the process of copying a gene's DNA sequence into an mRNA molecule. The enzyme that does this is RNA polymerase.

Template Strand vs. Coding Strand

Only one of the two DNA strands is read during transcription of any given gene. The strand that RNA polymerase actually reads is called the template strand (also called the antisense strand). RNA polymerase reads it in the 3′→5′ direction and builds the mRNA in the 5′→3′ direction, using complementary base pairing (except that RNA uses U instead of T). The other strand, which is not read but has the same sequence as the mRNA (with T instead of U), is called the coding strand (or sense strand).

Three Stages

Initiation: RNA polymerase binds to a specific DNA sequence called the promoter, which is located just upstream (before) the gene. In eukaryotes, transcription factors (helper proteins) must bind the promoter first before RNA polymerase can attach. The promoter tells RNA polymerase where to start and which strand to read.

Elongation: RNA polymerase moves along the template strand, unwinding the DNA and building the mRNA one nucleotide at a time, always in the 5′→3′ direction. Behind the polymerase, the DNA double helix re-forms.

Termination: RNA polymerase reaches a terminator sequence in the DNA, which signals the end of the gene. The mRNA is released, and the polymerase detaches.

Common Mistake
RNA polymerase does not need a primer. This is different from DNA polymerase, which always requires an RNA primer to start. RNA polymerase can initiate a new strand from scratch. This is a common exam question.

09mRNA Processing (Eukaryotes Only)

In eukaryotes, the mRNA that comes directly off the DNA template is called pre-mRNA. It must be processed before it leaves the nucleus. Three modifications occur:

5′ cap: A modified guanine nucleotide is added to the 5′ end of the mRNA. This cap protects the mRNA from degradation and helps ribosomes recognize and attach to it.

3′ poly-A tail: A string of 100 to 200 adenine nucleotides is added to the 3′ end. This protects the mRNA from enzymes that would chew it up from the end, and it helps export the mRNA from the nucleus.

Splicing: The pre-mRNA contains both exons (sequences that will be expressed, meaning they code for protein) and introns (intervening sequences that do not code for protein). A complex called the spliceosome cuts out the introns and joins the exons together to form the mature mRNA.

Memory Trick

"Exons are Expressed." "Introns are In the trash."

Key Point
Alternative splicing allows one gene to produce multiple different proteins by including different combinations of exons in the mature mRNA. This is how roughly 20,000 human genes can produce over 100,000 different proteins. It dramatically increases protein diversity without increasing the number of genes.
Prokaryotes Are Different
Prokaryotes do not have introns (generally), do not add a 5′ cap or poly-A tail, and do not process their mRNA. In fact, in prokaryotes, transcription and translation happen simultaneously because there is no nuclear membrane separating the DNA from the ribosomes. The ribosome can start translating the mRNA while it is still being transcribed.

10The Genetic Code

The mRNA is read in groups of three consecutive nucleotides. Each group of three is called a codon. Each codon specifies one amino acid (or a stop signal). Since there are 4 possible nucleotides at each position, and 3 positions per codon, there are 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 possible codons. These 64 codons specify only 20 amino acids plus 3 stop signals, so most amino acids are encoded by more than one codon. This redundancy is called degeneracy (it does not mean the code is defective; it means there are multiple codons for the same amino acid, usually differing at the third position).

Key Codons
Start codon: AUG (codes for methionine, sets the reading frame)
Stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA (do not code for any amino acid)

Properties of the Genetic Code

PropertyWhat It Means
UniversalNearly all organisms use the same code (minor exceptions in mitochondria and some microbes)

"U Are Annoying, U Are Gone, U Go Away" for UAA, UAG, UGA.

11Translation

Translation is the process of reading the mRNA codons and assembling a chain of amino acids (a polypeptide) that will fold into a protein. It occurs at ribosomes, which are molecular machines made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins.

The Players

mRNA: Carries the codon sequence from the gene. Read 5′→3′.

tRNA (transfer RNA): Small RNA molecules that serve as adapters. One end carries an anticodon (a three-nucleotide sequence complementary to the mRNA codon). The other end carries the corresponding amino acid. The enzyme that attaches the correct amino acid to its tRNA is called aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. There is a different synthetase for each of the 20 amino acids, and each one is highly specific.

Ribosome: Has three binding sites for tRNA: the A site (aminoacyl, where incoming tRNA arrives), P site (peptidyl, where the growing chain is held), and E site (exit, where spent tRNA leaves).

Three Stages

Initiation: The small ribosomal subunit binds to the mRNA and scans for the start codon (AUG). The initiator tRNA (carrying methionine) binds to AUG. The large ribosomal subunit then joins, forming the complete ribosome.

Elongation: The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time. At each codon, a tRNA with the matching anticodon enters the A site, a peptide bond forms between the amino acid in the A site and the growing chain in the P site, and the ribosome shifts one codon forward (translocation). The spent tRNA exits through the E site.

Termination: When the ribosome reaches a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA), no tRNA recognizes it. Instead, a release factor protein enters the A site, causing the ribosome to release the completed polypeptide chain and disassemble.

Cross-Disciplinary Bridge
The peptide bonds formed during translation are the same covalent bonds you encounter in biochemistry and organic chemistry. Each peptide bond is formed by a dehydration synthesis reaction (a water molecule is released when two amino acids are linked). Hydrolysis (adding water) breaks peptide bonds during protein digestion. The same reactions from the water module appear here in a completely different context.

12Mutations

A mutation is any change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA. Mutations can occur during replication (copying errors), from exposure to mutagens (UV light, chemicals, radiation), or spontaneously. Whether a mutation has an effect depends on what it changes and where.

Point Mutations (Single Nucleotide Changes)

TypeWhat HappensEffect
SilentCodon changes but still codes for the same amino acidNo effect on protein (possible because of code degeneracy)
MissenseCodon changes to code for a different amino acidMay or may not affect protein function (depends on the amino acid change and its location)
NonsenseCodon changes to a stop codonPremature termination; usually produces a nonfunctional, truncated protein

Frameshift Mutations

Insertions (extra nucleotides added) and deletions (nucleotides removed) shift the reading frame of every codon downstream. Because codons are read in a fixed frame of three with no gaps, adding or removing one or two nucleotides changes every codon after the mutation. Frameshifts are usually devastating because the entire amino acid sequence downstream is wrong.

Reading Frame Analogy

Original sentence (read in groups of 3): THE CAT ATE THE RAT

Delete the first E (frameshift): THC ATA TET HER AT_

Every "word" after the deletion is garbled. That is what a frameshift does to codons.

Key Point
Insertions or deletions of exactly 3 nucleotides (or multiples of 3) add or remove whole codons without shifting the reading frame. These are less damaging than frameshifts because the rest of the protein sequence remains correct, though the inserted or deleted amino acid(s) may still affect function.
Cross-Disciplinary Bridge
Mutations connect molecular biology to genetics (inheritance of genetic diseases), evolution (mutations are the raw material for natural selection), medicine (cancer results from accumulated mutations in growth-control genes), and pharmacology (some drugs target mutant proteins specifically). Sickle cell disease, for example, results from a single missense mutation: one nucleotide change converts a glutamic acid to a valine in the hemoglobin protein, altering its shape and function.
